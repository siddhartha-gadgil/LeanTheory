Introduction

AI for mathematics, and for science

  • Artificial Intelligence systems have become very powerful at mathematics.
  • It is natural to hope to use this, and other capabilities of AI, to accelerate scientific discovery, including engineering and mathematics.
  • To do this on a large scale we need high reliability and autonomy.
  • AI-discovered proofs are checked by translating them into computer-checkable proofs, mainly in the Lean prover.
  • The translation is also done by AI: autoformalization.

Abstraction and mathematics

Knowledge depends on abstraction

All knowledge beyond cataloguing facts (“stamp collection”) depends on abstraction: hiding details not relevant to what we seek to understand.

Newton’s laws give the trajectory of a point mass. We ignore

This is sometimes at the cost of simplifying, or taking an ideal model of, what we are studying.

Abstraction in engineering

Abstraction is most explicit in modular systems, for instance in software engineering.

  • We specify the properties we are concerned with, and study the system, or its idealised version, in terms of those properties.
  • What we deduce applies to every system for which the abstraction is valid.
  • Our attention is focussed on what matters for the questions at hand.

Abstraction and mathematics

Mathematics turns this on its head

  • Mathematics considers fully abstracted systems.
  • We define objects as those satisfying some properties (axioms), and think of them as real objects.
  • Each new abstraction gives new real objects, which we study and abstract further, based on what we discover and the techniques we use.
  • This leads to a tower of abstractions.
Naturesystems we observe and build
Modelsidealised systems, by their properties
Axiomsobjects defined by properties
Abstractionsof what we discover about them
…and so on, up the tower

Applicability of mathematics

Where mathematics falls short

  • Abstracting nature to obtain mathematics usually simplifies, leaving a gap between the empirical and the mathematical.
  • Even when the match is very good, simulations, numerics and experiments are often used instead of proofs.
  • The reason: the mathematics is too hard, too tedious, or both.
This limitation is what we can hope to overcome, with AI greatly accelerating mathematical discovery.

Model example: the design of fusion reactors called stellarators. Going ahead, I hope to build, and invite others to build, many smaller but interesting and useful mathematical theories.

Example: rational solutions of Diophantine equations

Diophantine equations

A polynomial equation with integer coefficients, such as $x^2 + y^2 = z^2$. When does it have a solution in integers?

  • Checking a candidate is easy, so searching finds a solution if there is one.
  • But we don’t know when to give up. For $x^2 - 61y^2 = 1$ the smallest positive solution is $$x = 1{,}766{,}319{,}049,\quad y = 226{,}153{,}980.$$

Ruling out solutions

Methods range from the elementary to the very deep.

Elementary: work modulo 4. Squares are $0$ or $1 \pmod 4$, so

$$x^2 + y^2 \not\equiv 3 \pmod 4,$$

and $x^2 + y^2 = 3$ has no integer solutions.

Example: rational solutions of Diophantine equations

Is there an algorithm?

1900

Hilbert’s 10th problem: is there an algorithm to decide if a Diophantine equation has a solution?

1930s

Church and Turing make “algorithm” precise, so the question becomes precise.

1948

Tarski: yes for real solutions.

1970

Davis, Putnam, Robinson, Matiyasevich: no for integer solutions.

2026

An October 6 OpenAI preprint: no for rational solutions, which had been left open.

Example: rational solutions of Diophantine equations

The idea of the proof

  1. Suppose an algorithm decides whether an equation has a rational solution.
  2. Use it to build a sequence of finite tests, such that integer solutions exist if and only if all the tests pass.
  3. Alternately plug in candidate integer solutions and carry out tests:
try candidate 1run test 1try candidate 2run test 2try candidate 3run test 3…one of these must succeed

Either a candidate is a solution or a test fails, so this decides integer solvability. That contradicts the 1970 theorem.

Lean Prover

An example, and why we can trust what Lean checks.

Lean prover: an example

There are infinitely many primes

/-- A prime is a number larger than 1 with only trivial divisors -/
def IsPrime (n : Nat) := 1 < n ∧ ∀ k, 1 < k → k < n → ¬ k ∣ n

/-- We find arbitrarily large (and thus infinitely many) primes:
`n! + 1` has a prime factor larger than `n` -/
theorem InfinitudeOfPrimes : ∀ n, ∃ p > n, IsPrime p := by
  intro n
  have : 1 < n ! + 1 := by grind [factorial_pos]
  obtain ⟨p, hp, _⟩ := exists_prime_factor (n ! + 1) this
  suffices ¬p ≤ n by grind
  intro (_ : p ≤ n)
  have : 1 < p := hp.1
  have : p ∣ n ! := dvd_factorial n p ‹p ≤ n› (by grind)
  have := Nat.dvd_sub ‹p ∣ n ! + 1› ‹p ∣ n !›
  grind [Nat.add_sub_cancel_left, Nat.dvd_one]
  • The definition and statement are close to how a mathematician writes them.
  • The proof is Euclid’s: a prime factor $p$ of $n! + 1$ cannot be at most $n$, since then $p \mid 1$.
  • The full file also defines the factorial and proves that every $n > 1$ has a prime factor.
  • Lean checks every step; grind is an automated tactic.

  • The kernel is like the part of a chess engine that checks whether moves are legal.
  • Correctness of checked proofs depends only on the kernel, not on all of Lean.
  • Independent kernels exist in other languages, so an invalid proof is accepted only if it passes all of them. Other checks, such as model building, have also been implemented.

Trust and verification with Lean

But is it the right statement?

Even if a proof is correct, it may be proving the wrong thing:

  • different from what we intend, in mathematics;
  • mismatched with reality, in science.

This comes from errors in definitions or statements, and we need to guard against them.

Part 4

Autonomous Verification of Models and Definitions

Checking definitions is easier than checking proofs, but manual checking limits scale. Three principles reduce errors with modest manual intervention.

Autonomous verification

Three principles

🎯

Proof challenges

Many statements that must hold if a definition is correct. Proving them all raises our confidence in the definition.

◉

Small kernels

The defining model of a domain is isolated in a modest amount of code, tested intensively and inspected by hand.

⚙

Swiss-watch verification

In an interlocking theory, errors cause failures rather than cancelling out, so the theory is largely self-correcting.

Autonomous verification

Proof challenges

  • To test a definition, use AI to generate many statements that hold if the definition is correct.
  • Generate them following sanity-check recipes well known to mathematicians: special cases, degenerate cases, examples and non-examples.
  • Being able to prove all of them greatly increases our confidence in the definition.
-- If `IsPrime` is right, all of these must be provable

theorem two_prime : IsPrime 2                -- an example

theorem one_not_prime : ¬ IsPrime 1          -- a degenerate case

theorem four_not_prime : ¬ IsPrime 4         -- a non-example

theorem exists_prime_factor :                -- a characterizing property
    ∀ n, 1 < n → ∃ k, IsPrime k ∧ k ∣ n

Autonomous verification

Small kernels

  • Lean itself has a small kernel. In the same way, we isolate the key defining model of our domain into a modest amount of code.
  • Other results are verified in terms of this domain kernel.
  • The kernel itself is intensively tested with proof challenges, and can even be inspected by hand.

Autonomous verification

Swiss-watch verification

  • In a complex interlocking design, such as a Swiss watch or a mathematical theory, an error causes failures: jamming, or compilation errors.
  • A few errors are highly unlikely to cancel. Instead they cause worse failures.
  • So interlocking theories built on stress-tested kernels are largely self-correcting.
The gears jam when the second point appears: one wrong tooth stops the whole mechanism.

Part 5

Building Theories: a Plug-and-Play Setup

The principles packaged as a template repository, used with an AI coding agent.

Building theories

The LeanTheory template

  • The principles above are packaged in a template repository, LeanTheory ↗.
  • To build a theory in some domain, create a fresh repository from the template, named for the topic, and work in it with an AI coding agent.
  • The repository contains instructions for the agent, called skills, so most interaction is in natural language.
  • Little expertise in Lean, or in setting up AI agents, is needed.

Building theories

Developing theory, not just translating it

We begin with goals, open questions, partial approaches, sources, calculations and design problems, tracked as a graph:

  • Goals: desired mathematical, scientific or engineering outcomes.
  • Questions: unresolved issues, the answer we seek or a step towards a goal.
  • Notes: units of work, such as a literature summary, a definition, a conjecture, a counterexample, a proof or a formalization.

Working on a note need not end in Lean code. It may produce new theory, split a question, expose an obstacle or refute an approach.

Each note records what it depends on, what it is expected to produce, its difficulty and the barriers to completing it.

Only verified results can be relied on by later work. Consequences of a conjecture may be explored, as long as the dependence is recorded. This guards against two failures of long AI runs:

Research drift

Losing the motivating question, repeating abandoned approaches, or silently changing definitions.

Validity drift

Treating a plausible argument, a sampled computation or a cited result as if it had been checked under the exact hypotheses needed.

Building theories

Layers of a theory

The Lean code is organized in layers, separating the model from what is deduced from it.

Laws of nature and known facts are hypotheses that theorems explicitly assume, never axioms.

Building theories

A worked example: the harmonic oscillator

  • Kernel: Newton’s law $m\ddot x = -kx$ for a mass on a spring.
  • Theory: the energy $\tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12 kx^2$ is conserved.
  • Certificates: exact checks for 21 initial states, giving the bound $3x(t)^2 \le 1$ for all time.
The domain kernel is not Lean’s kernel. Lean verifies the consequences of the model we encoded. Whether the model applies is for experts and experiments: the gap between the empirical and the mathematical.

With $m = k = 1$: the mass moves, while its state $(x, \dot x)$ stays on a circle of constant energy, inside the certified bound.

Building theories

Sanity checks and audits

Every law in the kernel is tested by at least three proof challenges. For the oscillator:

Degenerate case

With zero stiffness, $k = 0$, the oscillator is a free particle.

Concrete instance

$x(t) = \cos t$ is a solution, so the law can be satisfied and theorems about it are not vacuously true.

Counterexample

$x(t) = e^t$ is not a solution, so the law rules something out.

  • This is a baseline. Further checks target each law’s risks: singularities, symmetries, conservation laws, scaling, boundary behaviour, order of quantifiers.
  • Every error found later becomes a permanent check.
  • Audits are strict and include old code: code is not trusted just because it compiles. The first command in a repository audits the whole kernel.

Getting started

PlasmaLean

A library for stellarator design

  • PlasmaLean ↗ is a library of Lean and AI-assisted mathematics aimed at the design of a stellarator.
  • It is the project from which the LeanTheory template was extracted, so it follows the model just described.
  • Its website ↗ has background on stellarators, a handbook, progress slides and an exposition suite.

Stellarators

A magnetic bottle needs twisted field lines

Fusion needs deuterium and tritium at over a hundred million degrees: a plasma, kept away from the walls by a toroidal magnetic bottle. In a simple toroidal field, ions and electrons drift apart and hit the wall. The cure is field lines that twist, measured by the rotational transform $\iota$.

ion, drifting up electron, drifting down magnetic field lines Illustrative animation, not a computed orbit.
Left: with no twist, the vertical drift carries each particle straight out to the wall. Right: as the particle follows a twisted field line it spends time at the top and the bottom of the torus, so the drift averages out and the orbit stays inside.

Stellarators

Tokamaks and stellarators

Tokamak: the twist comes from a large current, millions of amperes, driven through the plasma itself. This current brings instabilities and sudden disruptions.
Stellarator: the twist comes from shaped external coils, with little or no plasma current. The field cannot be symmetric about the axis of the torus, so stellarators are genuinely three-dimensional.
Both pictures are schematic. The stellarator shape is a rotating ellipse with a helical axis and five field periods, surrounded by non-planar modular coils.

Stellarators

Hard, and mathematically rich

  • A generic three-dimensional field confines particles poorly, so stellarators must be carefully designed.
  • Field lines should lie on nested flux surfaces: invariant tori.
  • Small perturbations break some tori into islands and chaos. KAM theory says which tori survive.
  • Grad’s conjecture concerns whether non-symmetric plasma equilibria with nested flux surfaces exist.

Where field lines cross a cross-section, modelled by Hénon’s area-preserving map. Closed curves are invariant tori, chains are islands, and scattered points are chaotic field lines.

PlasmaLean

The stellarator contract

The goal is a single candidate design satisfying five clauses, with the same objects witnessing all of them.

ClauseRequirement
C1  current-free twistRotational transform $\iota \ge \iota_{\min} > 0$ from the external field, with zero or very small net plasma current. This rules out tokamaks.
C2  omnigeneitySmall transport of thermal ions and electrons out of the plasma.
C3  stabilityStability against the main magnetohydrodynamic instabilities, at a positive plasma pressure.
C4  existenceNested flux surfaces, on which field lines lie, with force balance.
C5  performanceThe core stays above a burn temperature, e.g. 10 keV, from a balance of heating and losses.

Being non-symmetric is a consequence of these clauses, not a goal. A non-symmetric result with no other content is mathematical infrastructure, not design progress.

PlasmaLean

Kernel, and errors caught by audits

~900

lines in the domain kernel, in six modules: force balance, vacuum fields, simplified one-dimensional plasma and heat laws, radial transport, fusion power balance.

600k+

lines of Lean in the library built on it, using only Lean’s standard axioms. Each law is a hypothesis, never an axiom.

Force balance

$J \times B = g$ holds for every $B$ and $J$ if $g$ is unconstrained: meaningless until $g$ is proved to be the pressure gradient.

Current-free twist

An early C1 used a surface that could be degenerate, so every field passed. Caught by an audit, kept as a regression check, fixed using Ampère’s law.

Plasma composition

The plasma law allowed contradictory impurity and fuel values. Now covered by regression checks.

PlasmaLean

Results, as of early November 2026

  • Lead candidate: an exact, purely rational vacuum field of a kind first found by Dommaschk, with two field periods. Found by untrusted numerics, then certified in Lean: a KAM theorem with explicit constants gives two nested invariant tori, with zero net current and $|\iota| \ge 0.2386$ on both (C1), and a certified small deviation from quasi-axisymmetry, a symmetry of particle motion that helps C2.
  • Confinement for all time: an earlier exact vacuum field has a KAM-certified invariant torus, so every field line starting inside stays inside forever. Certain high-energy alpha particles produced by fusion are also confined for all time.
  • Coils: concrete polygonal coils give field lines near the axis certified to stay confined for 52 circuits. Another certificate bounds field errors from manufacturing tolerances, for a test coil pair.
  • Grad’s conjecture: an explicit non-symmetric equilibrium of Landreman satisfies, in Lean, the hypotheses of a counterexample.
  • Test beds: stability for a band of a Landreman equilibrium and for a tokamak equilibrium (Solov’ev), and a tokamak model whose core stays above 10 keV for every supplied solution of a transport model.

PlasmaLean

How it was built

  • Built almost entirely by AI agents, with many ideas contributed by Vamsi Pingali and Animesh Kuley. It used the workflow above: a research graph of goals, questions and notes, Lean as the validity gate, and strict audits.
  • Heavy numerics run on a workstation as untrusted oracles; their certificates are re-checked before they are merged.
  • Large checks are written as explicit proof terms that Lean’s kernel verifies.
Work produced in this way can be queried and understood, and ideas at various levels can be added to guide it.

The exposition suite includes a “Rosetta stone” translating stellarator physics into geometry, a guide to auditing the kernel in ten minutes, and challenges inviting pure mathematicians to contribute.
